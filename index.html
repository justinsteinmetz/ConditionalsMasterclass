The classic traps

  • No will in the if-part of a prediction: If it will rain → If it rains.
  • No would in the if-part: If I would have time → If I had time. (Polite requests are the exception: If you would wait here, …)
  • No would have in the if-part: If I would have known → If I had known.
  • No double negative with unless: Unless you don't → Unless you.
  • in case is not if: it happens before the problem.

Big idea: the less real the situation, the further back the verb travels. The grammar shows how real the speaker is treating the situation; context tells you the actual facts.

Running the finale with no extra preparation

  • Groups of four, one role each: Historian, Strategist, Futurist, Sceptic. In a group of three, the Strategist also takes the Futurist's role. Pairs can share Historian/Strategist and Futurist/Sceptic.
  • Each group chooses one of six cases on the Results page. The facts they need are on the card, so no research is required. The facts are deliberately brief and widely documented; invite students to challenge or check any detail.
  • The tangible product is the Counterfactual Report (fields on the page, with a “Copy report” button). Groups can paste it into your preferred tool or present straight from the screen.
  • Presentation and questions: each group presents its report. A different group must ask at least one follow-up question using the Sceptic's starters, and the presenters answer with a conditional.
  • Debrief: Was it the moment, or the system? Whose version of events gets told as the “what if”?

Listen for while circulating

  • If I will… (first conditional): ask “Where does the future live in this sentence?”
  • If I would have… (third): ask “Which clause is allowed to have would?”
  • Mixed: ask “Which word tells you the result is now?”
  • Unless … not: have them swap unless for if … not and say it aloud.

Grammar notes

  • The third conditional imagines a past that was different. The usual inference (“I didn't revise and didn't pass”) comes from context and convention, not from the grammar alone.
  • Unless means “except if”. It usually matches if … not but not always: I'll be upset if she doesn't come is not the same as …unless she comes.
  • Otherwise means if not. It can join two clauses or begin a new sentence.
  • If and when overlap for general truths; when expects the event, if treats it as a possibility.
  • The exit-ticket check is a heuristic. It can miss errors and can flag correct sentences, and it says so when it is unsure.

Stretch and follow-up

  • Discuss structurally: why do societies use the third conditional for blame (“If they had listened…”)? Who gets to write the counterfactual history?
  • Next lesson: inversion (Had I known…, Were I you…, Should you need…) and wish / if only.
